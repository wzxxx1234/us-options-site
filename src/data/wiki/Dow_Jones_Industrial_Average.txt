The '''Dow Jones Industrial Average''' ('''DJIA'''), '''Dow Jones''', or simply the '''Dow''' ({{IPAc-en|'|d|aʊ}}), is a [[stock market index]] tracking the stock performance of 30 large companies listed on [[stock exchange]]s in the [[United States]]. It is named after founder [[Charles Dow]] and his business associate, statistician [[Edward Jones (statistician)|Edward Jones]]. It is maintained by [[S&P Dow Jones Indices]] and its components are selected by a committee.

The index is a [[price-weighted index]];<ref name=data/> stocks with the highest price per share have the highest weighting in the index. The value of the index is the sum of the stock prices of the companies included in the index, divided by a factor, which is approximately 0.168 {{as of|2026|07|lc=y}}. The factor is changed whenever a constituent company undergoes a [[stock split]] so that the value of the index is unaffected by the stock split.

Because the DJIA only includes 30 components, many of which are highly correlated during periods of decline,<ref>{{cite journal | author-link1=Tobias Preis |first1=Tobias |last1=Preis |first2=Dror Y. |last2=Kenett |author-link3=H. Eugene Stanley |first3=H. Eugene |last3=Stanley |author-link4=Dirk Helbing |first4=Dirk |last4=Helbing |author-link5=Eshel Ben-Jacob|first5=Eshel |last5=Ben-Jacob | title=Quantifying the Behavior of Stock Correlations Under Market Stress | journal=[[Scientific Reports]] | volume= 2 | article-number=752 | year=2012 | doi=10.1038/srep00752  | doi-access=free| pmid=23082242 | pmc=3475344| bibcode= 2012NatSR...2..752P}}</ref> and the fact that the components with the highest price per share have an undue weighting ([[Goldman Sachs]] has the highest weighting in the index and the index performed extremely poorly during the [[2008 financial crisis]] due to the drop of then-component [[AIG]]), the DJIA is not considered representative of the U.S. economy and products tracking the index are not recommended as long-term investments by most financial advisors. However, in the long run, the index has performed in line with the broader [[S&P 500]].<ref>{{Cite web | last=Bates | first=Alex |title=Head-to-Head: Dow vs. S&P 500 (And The Shocking Results) | url=https://www.stlouistrust.com/insights/head-to-head-dow-vs-sp-500-and-the-shocking-results/ | website=St. Louis Trust & Family Office |date=2023-11-03}}</ref>

Products linked to the DJIA include [[index fund]]s ([[exchange-traded fund]]s/ETFs and [[mutual funds]]) as well as [[Derivative (finance)|derivative]]s ([[Option (finance)|options]] and [[futures contract]]s), which are available for trading in many countries with the goal of replicating the performance of the index. Also available are modified index funds; they replicate the performance of the index with modifications such as the use of [[covered call]] strategies, equal weighting, performance buffers, [[Leverage (finance)|leverage]], inclusion of only growth or value stocks, or exclusion of certain sectors, all with the goal of changing the risk/return and [[Yield (finance)|yield]].

The ten components with the largest [[dividend yield]]s are referred to as the [[Dogs of the Dow]].

==History==
In 1884, [[Charles Dow]] created his first stock average featuring 11 stocks, mostly railroads, which later became the [[Dow Jones Transportation Average]]. It was published in his newsletter, ''Customer's Afternoon Letter'', a daily two-page financial news bulletin which was the precursor to ''[[The Wall Street Journal]]''. On February 16, 1885, the Dow Jones Average (DJA) launched as a 14-stock index.  On January 2, 1886, the number of stocks represented in what is now the ''Dow Jones Transportation Average'' dropped from 14 to 12, as the [[Central Pacific Railroad]] and [[Central Railroad of New Jersey]] were removed. On May 26, 1896,<ref name=data/> the Dow Jones Industrial Average debuted with 12 industrial stocks at a starting value of 40.94.<ref name=fineon/> The index's 12 original companies were:<ref>{{Cite web |last=Terrell |first=Ellen |title=Research Guides: This Month in Business History: Dow Jones Industrial Average First Published |url=https://guides.loc.gov/this-month-in-business-history/may/djia-first-published |access-date=2026-10-22 |website=guides.loc.gov |language=en}}</ref>

* American Cotton Oil Company
* American Sugar Company
* American Tobacco Company

==Computation==
[[File:DJIA Trading Volume.png|thumb|upright=1|DJIA monthly trading volume in shares from 1929 to 2012]]
The DJIA is computed as the sum of the prices of all thirty stocks divided by a [[divisor]], the Dow Divisor. The divisor is adjusted in case of stock splits, spinoffs or similar structural changes, to ensure that such events do not in themselves alter the numerical value of the DJIA. Early on, the initial divisor was composed of the original number of component companies; this initially made the DJIA a simple [[arithmetic]] average. The present divisor, after many adjustments, is less than one, making the index larger than the sum of the prices of the components. That is:
<math display=block> \text{DJIA} = {\sum p \over d}</math>
where '''''p''''' are the prices of the component stocks and '''''d''''' is the ''Dow Divisor''.

Events such as [[stock split]]s or changes in the list of the companies composing the index alter the sum of the component prices. In these cases, in order to avoid discontinuity in the index, the Dow Divisor is updated so that the quotations right before and after the event coincide:

<math display=block> \text{DJIA} = {\sum p_\text{old} \over d_\text{old} } = {\sum p_\text{new} \over d_\text{new} }.</math>

{{As of|2026|06|29|since=y|df=us|post=,}} the Dow Divisor is '''0.16824816528350'''<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.barrons.com/market-data/market-lab?mod=md_subnav |title=Market Lab | work=[[Barron's]] |date=October 15, 2024}}</ref><ref>{{cite news |url=https://www.morningstar.com/news/marketwatch/2024022666/amazons-stock-could-lose-to-walgreens-this-year-if-the-dow-jinx-holds |title=Amazon's stock could lose to Walgreens' this year if the Dow jinx holds | work=[[Morningstar, Inc.]] |date=February 26, 2024}}</ref> and every $1 change in price in a particular stock within the average equates to a '''5.943601''' (or 1 '''÷''' 0.16824816528350) point movement.
